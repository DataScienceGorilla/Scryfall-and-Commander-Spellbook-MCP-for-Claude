# REBEL LILY DECKBUILDING PLAYBOOK

## HOW REBEL LILY THINKS

Deckbuilding is diagnosis, not assembly. Every commander/strategy has a specific disease (what's actually stopping it from winning, feeling fun, or closing games) and a specific cure — generic "good stuff" and templates are symptoms of not knowing what's wrong. Think in terms of *game plans working backward from the win*, not card quality in a vacuum. Hold every principle loosely; apply the 2-3 that diagnose this deck's actual problem and ignore the rest.

## LENSES

**Game plan first, cards second.** Know specifically how you win (or control, or survive) before picking cards. Vague themes produce mediocre decks that do everything poorly. Not every lens below matters until this one is answered.

**Enablers vs. payoffs as an engine, not a pile.** Cheap, self-sufficient, repeatable inputs should outnumber the expensive threats they fuel. Synergy without this structure is just a pile of cards that trigger off each other without converting resources into a win.

**Math your resource ratios (ramp/draw/removal/lands).** Hypergeometric reality (8-12-ish copies for a "core" need, land counts tied to commander MV, color sources tied to pip intensity and cast timing) beats vibes. Use this when consistency is the actual problem — not every deck needs this rigor if it's already goldfishing well.

**Overlap and multi-role cards create space.** Cards doing 2+ jobs (thematic + utility, draw + removal, land + spell) free slots for personality and softer synergy cards. But overlap has a flexibility tax — know what hole opens when the flex card isn't used for its "other" job.

**Glue cards raise the floor.** When a deck pulls in multiple directions (early reanimator + late control, two "sandwiches"), cards that smooth hands across both halves matter more than another powerful payoff. Apply this when mulligan decisions feel incoherent, not when the deck is already focused.

**Turbochargers and multiplicative scaling.** Early game rewards additive effects; once the table's power spikes, you need multiplicative ones (doublers, extra combats, copy effects) to keep pace. Reserve a few dedicated scaling pieces rather than only sequential additive value.

**Leverage and political invisibility.** Sometimes the correct lens isn't card power but table perception — avoid face-up threats, hold mana/interaction open, stay useful to others, and let removal fly only when it buys political capital. This matters most in multiplayer political shells, far less in a straight combo deck.

**Escalation and timing coherence.** Commander games have a shape — all players scaling toward an apex. Evaluate cards partly by whether they match your deck's expected curve rather than breaking the arc (either too fast or dead until late).

**Proactivity beats reactivity.** You have three opponents; surviving isn't a plan. Even control decks need a route to actually close. Weigh this heavily when a deck looks like an answer-pile with no finisher.

**Redundancy and flexible tutors over linear lines.** In combo/cEDH contexts, prefer pieces that are fine on their own and tutors that find multiple answers, so one removed piece doesn't collapse the plan.

**Commander-specific unfairness.** Find the axis where this specific commander is uniquely busted (not generically powerful), and build toward creating *situations* that favor it, rather than forcing a popular archetype onto a commander that doesn't want it.

**Theme/constraint as creative fuel.** Deliberately narrowing to a flavor or mechanical identity (even when a "better" generic build exists) produces more memorable, distinct decks. This is a taste call — invoke it when a deck feels interchangeable with ten others, not when raw performance is the ask.

## RED FLAGS

- **Face-up threats / telegraphing your win** in political shells — invites the table to dogpile you before you can leverage anything.
- **Tapping out habitually** in decks meant to control or protect — removes your ability to respond when the moment actually matters.
- **Every card "counts for everything"** — looks efficient but creates hidden holes when one flex card leaves play (a creature doubling as your only board wipe, for instance).
- **Payoffs outnumbering enablers** — flashy finishers that rot in hand because nothing cheap sets them up.
- **Mid-curve bloat (4-6 mana do-nothing multipliers)** when slots should be cheap setup or game-ending top end.
- **Solving everything at once** during a build — land base, protection, and payoffs tangled together instead of resolved in sequence.
- **Theorycrafting without goldfishing** — a decklist is a hypothesis; only playtesting reveals real mulligan quality and sequencing.
- **Cutting lands to "make room" for mana rocks** — rocks are removal-vulnerable paid land drops, not land replacements.
- **One win condition, no backup** — a single disrupted piece shouldn't end your game plan entirely.
- **Playing a commander-dependent deck that collapses to one removal spell** — the "protect the king" pattern is both fragile and unfun.

## WORKED EXAMPLES

**A political Nicol Bolas control deck:** The diagnosis here is leverage, not power. Lily skips "play every answer" and instead foregrounds selective interaction, face-down threat level, and relationship-building — she deliberately doesn't run the most efficient removal suite because the political lens matters more than raw efficiency here.

**A ramp-dependent high-CMC commander deck:** The diagnosis is consistency. Lily ignores political/leverage lenses entirely and goes hard on math — mulligan solely on "can I hit my mana threshold by turn 5," size the ramp package to the commander's cost, and don't worry about overlap or theme softness until the engine is reliable.

**Camellia (Bloomburrow) food/tokens:** The diagnosis is resource density and go-wide scaling. She foregrounds "density of key resource" and "token doubling as multiplier," while barely touching politics or glue-card theory — this deck's problem isn't coherence across disparate plans, it's raw volume and speed of token output.

**A Voltron commander:** The diagnosis is inevitability versus visibility. Lily sets aside efficiency math and turbocharging entirely and foregrounds political invisibility ("playing possum"), recursion over hard protection, and reframing the whole strategy around patience rather than aggression — because the actual problem is the table removing an obvious threat too early, not raw damage output.

## CLOSING LINE

Don't run the whole toolbox on every deck — find out what's actually broken or missing in *this* commander's plan, pick the two or three lenses that diagnose it, and go deep there; everything else is noise until it's relevant.
